The brain reads your markdown and answers questions about it, citing the exact line. Here is what happens in between, and which command drives each part.

Why searching for words isn't enough

Ask "how do merchants get their money faster?" when the document that answers it says "settlement latency compresses working capital" — not one word matches, and ordinary search finds nothing. Search a common word instead and you get four hundred hits. Too few, then too many.

The brain solves both by never relying on one method. It looks for your question in three completely different ways at once, then combines the answers.

Step 1 · Documents become index cards

A whole document is the wrong unit. A 12,000-word paper might answer your question in one paragraph; return the file and you've handed someone a haystack.

So each document is cut into chunks of about 240 words — index cards. Every card remembers its document, the heading it sat under, and the line it starts at. That is how a citation like strategy/uk.md:365 is possible at all.

Cards are cut at heading boundaries first, so one never straddles two unrelated sections, and consecutive cards overlap by 36 words, so a sentence falling on a cut still survives whole on one side of it.

/brain-indexDoes the cutting — walks every connected repository and rebuilds the cards for anything whose text changed.
/brain-sourceDecides which repositories get walked.

Step 2 · Three ways to find a card

One — the exact words

Which cards literally contain your words? This uses BM25, a fifty-year-old scoring rule that is still excellent. Two ideas do the work: more mentions beat fewer, and a rare word counts for far more than a common one. Matching a company's registered name is strong evidence; matching "the" is none.

Unbeatable for names, codes and exact phrases. Useless for a paraphrase.

Two — the meaning

A model called bge-m3 reads a card and turns it into 1,024 numbers — the card's position in an imaginary space with 1,024 directions. The model was trained so that passages meaning similar things land near each other, with no words in common required.

Your question becomes 1,024 numbers the same way, and finding relevant cards is simply: which positions sit closest? That is why the working-capital passage answers the merchants question.

Because bge-m3 was trained on many languages at once, a Spanish document lands near an English question about the same thing. Ask in Spanish, get the Spanish documents — no translation step anywhere.

/brain-setupDownloads the model that does the converting. Until it has run, this second way is unavailable and the brain says so.
/brain-indexComputes the 1,024 numbers for every new or changed card — the slow part of a first index.

Three — the connections

The third way ignores your question's words and its meaning. It looks at what your documents are about and how those things relate: a network of entities — companies, people, products, decisions — joined by the relationships your notes state.

This catches what the other two structurally cannot. One passage says "the regulatory application"; another says "the consultant's day rate". Nothing links them by words or meaning. The network knows both attach to the same pilot.

/brain-enrichBuilds this network. Without it the brain still answers using the first two ways — you just lose the third.
entityQueries the network directly. Use it when your question names one thing.

Step 3 · Combining three answers into one

Three methods produce three ranked lists whose scores are in unrelated units — 14.2, 0.81, 0.06. Averaging them is meaningless.

So the scores are thrown away and only the rankings vote. This is reciprocal rank fusion, and it behaves like a ranked-choice election: each method votes for a card with strength 1 / (60 + rank). A card ranked well by two methods beats a card ranked first by only one — which is right, because agreement between independent methods is stronger evidence than one method's enthusiasm.

Roughly 55% the fused vote, 25% how brightly the graph lights the card, 10% how much you trust its source, 10% how recent it is. Superseded documents lose more than half their score, and no document may take more than two slots — so you get a spread of evidence, not one file five times.

/brain-askRuns this entire pipeline and answers with the passages it ends up with.
/brain-sourceSets the trust weight in that 10% slice. Your anchor repository sits at 1.0; sources added later normally sit below it.

Step 4 · How the graph moves an answer

Picture the entities and cards as dots joined by lines. Your question drops light on the dots it obviously touches — the cards the first two searches liked, plus any entity you named. That light then spreads outward along the connections, dimmer at each hop, for seven rounds.

Cards in a well-lit neighbourhood score higher even though your words never appear in them. This is personalised PageRank — the idea that ranked the early web, except the starting light is placed by your question rather than spread evenly.

/brain-viewThe Graph tab above draws this network — nodes sized by how often they appear, click one to isolate its neighbourhood.

Where the network comes from

Something has to read the cards and write down what is in them. Batches are handed to Claude with a strict instruction: list the entities this passage actually names and the relationships it actually states — invent nothing, bring in nothing from outside.

Two choices make this practical. It runs as subagents in your own session, so it costs time and context rather than money. And every card is tracked by a fingerprint of its text, so a card that has not changed is never re-read — stop the job halfway and nothing is lost or repeated.

Extraction names things as the text spells them, so one company can arrive as several entries — an abbreviation and its expansion, a name and a transcript's misspelling. A later pass folds those together, but only when two independent extractions each called the other an alias. That conservative rule is what stops a product being swallowed by the company it is named after.

/brain-enrichRuns the extraction batch by batch. Safe to stop and resume.
merge_entitiesRuns the folding pass. Ask for the plan first — it shows exactly what it proposes to merge.

The layer above · answers no passage contains

Ask "what are our recurring risks?" and there is no card to find. The answer is spread across dozens.

So an algorithm called Leiden looks at the entity network and finds its communities — clusters that connect to each other far more than to everything else, the way friend groups emerge in a social network without anyone labelling them. Each cluster is summarised, and those summaries become searchable objects that still point back to the real files.

/brain-enrich --communitiesFinds the clusters and writes the summaries. Needs the entity network first.
search_themesRanks those summaries instead of passages — for when no single document holds the answer.

Why it stays fast

Everything expensive — turning a card into 1,024 numbers, having Claude read it — is stored against a fingerprint of that card's exact text. Re-indexing compares fingerprints and skips whatever has not moved. Edit one paragraph and only the cards that actually changed are recomputed.

A first full index of a few hundred thousand words takes tens of minutes. Every index after that takes seconds.

/brain-indexCheap to run often. When files move ahead of the index, the health strip at the top of this page says so.

The other direction · putting knowledge back

Tell the brain something worth keeping and it writes a real markdown file into your repository — dated, titled, tagged, with a confidence level and links to entities it recognises — then indexes it immediately.

That direction matters more than it sounds. Your markdown is the truth; the index is disposable. Delete the whole database and you lose an afternoon of recomputation, never any knowledge.

/brain-addWrites the note and indexes it, so the very next search finds it.
/brain-dreamReports contradictions, ageing facts and orphans across what you have written. It reports; it never edits.
